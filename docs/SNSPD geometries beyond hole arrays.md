# Stop perforating strips and start protecting edges

The hole-array approach has reached a dead end for this thesis goal. The reason is structural, not a matter of tuning. Holes can only add crowded, low-barrier sites inside the absorbing strip. Every design that has widened the gap between photon detection and dark counts in experiments does the reverse: it protects the weakest sites (edges, bends) and leaves the absorbing bulk sensitive, or makes it more sensitive. The student's own results already show this. I_sw scales with free width. Every device switches at the same ~7.1 µA/nm local sheet current. Weak seeds shift every geometry by the same amount. The one gain (i_50 about 0.02 lower) appears only with a loss-free reflecting hotspot in 56 nm channels, which looks like the known narrow-wire effect, and the unrun 56 nm control will most likely reproduce it. So they have not exhausted what is possible; they have exhausted the wrong lever. The next geometry should be a different class. It should be a straight strip with nominal-strength edge bands and a weakened, sensitised centre, built with py-tdgl's spatially varying ε(r). That is the TDGL analogue of demonstrated bank structures, local He-ion irradiation, thick bends and edge rails. A new geometry alone will not make the conclusion credible, though. A defect-free TDGL strip already switches at its depairing limit, so its deterministic I_sw cannot show the dark-count suppression the thesis wants. The thesis also needs a barrier-based dark-count estimate, an energy sink in the hotspot, a stricter detection criterion and a realistically defect-limited baseline. There is also a hard floor. Once edges are protected, bulk vortex-antivortex unbinding (about 0.76-0.87 I_dep in 2026 work) limits any planar geometry. Going past it requires material or hotspot changes that 2D TDGL can only mimic.

## Hole arrays only rescale the strip, so they cannot widen the window

Labels used throughout: *demonstrated* means measured on real devices; *simulated* means published TDGL/GL numerics; *theory* means analytic models; *student's result* means the thesis simulations described in the brief; *inference* marks reasoning in the research notes or in this report that no source states directly.

**The student's own numbers already contain the verdict.** Two of their findings are that I_sw per unit free width is the same in every geometry and that every device switches at the same local peak sheet current. Together these imply that crowding, measured against the current in the remaining free width, is nearly the same everywhere: 1.21 for the plain strip and about 1.25 for the zipper. The apparent rise to ~1.45 at hole sides is mostly the full-width mean re-expressed for a narrower conductor. The staggered columns leave a minimum free width of 173 nm, so for the zipper 1.21 × 200/173 ≈ **1.40**, against the measured 1.45 (inference; this assumes both factors are peak over full-width mean). The remaining few-percent gap is similar in size to the spread in the other two measures: I_sw per free width is about 1% lower for the zipper, and the peak current at switching about 2% higher. The holes move the weak site; they do not make it weaker or stronger. A second check makes the point sharper. For the stated parameters (ξ = 5 nm, λ = 175 nm, d = 9 nm), the GL depairing sheet current d·Φ0/(3√3πμ0λ²ξ) is **≈5.9 µA/nm**. The plain strip's mean sheet current at switching, 7.1/1.21, is also ≈5.9 µA/nm, within about 1% (inference, this report's arithmetic). So the ideal strip appears to switch at essentially its depairing current. This is what dirty-limit theory predicts for an ideal strip, where the vortex-entry barrier vanishes exactly at the depairing condition ([Kubo 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11518)). In this model no geometry can raise I_sw. Geometry can only lower it.

Theory and experiment both agree. In London theory, the vortex-entry barrier at any crowded point vanishes when the *local* sheet current reaches the straight-edge critical value, provided the radius of curvature is much larger than ξ ([Clem & Berggren 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.4881)). The 16 × 110 nm ellipses are in that regime at their long sides. Those sides have a radius of curvature of a²/b ≈ 380 nm, while the tips (b²/a ≈ 1.2 nm, below ξ) are current-stagnation points rather than crowding points (inference). This explains why the 7.1 µA/nm local criterion is universal. Experimentally, deliberate constrictions in NbN wires gave **I_sw proportional to constriction width**. The bias ratio needed for 0.1% detection efficiency equalled the width ratio (0.86, 0.75, 0.63), and the dark-count rate versus I_b/I_sw had the same slope for every constriction, differing only in intercept. The authors conclude that "dark counts in SNSPDs originate from constrictions" ([Zhang et al. 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2043)). The closest hole experiment used ~960 nm NbN strips with a single circular hole. A central hole gave I_c/I_dep ≈ **0.42-0.49**, against ~0.5 without a hole, and the hole changed how switching starts (vortex-antivortex nucleation at the hole wall) rather than making the strip more sensitive. A hole near, but not at, the edge gave the *lowest* I_c ([Vodolazov et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.02234)). The notes found no published experiment or simulation in which a perforated, antidot or width-modulated SNSPD lowers I_det/I_sw. That is a gap in the literature, not proof of impossibility, but it also means nobody has reported the effect the thesis is looking for.

The equal weak-seed shift fits the same picture. In all three geometries, seeded and unseeded switching end in the same final step: a vortex enters where the local sheet current is highest. A seed lowers that barrier by a roughly fixed amount, so the threshold shift is the same everywhere (inference). Experiments that do separate dark counts from photon counts put the two processes in *different places*. Below about 70 mT, the photon count rate of NbN meanders barely responds to magnetic field, while the dark-count rate follows the meander's I_c(H). The authors conclude that dark counts come from "fluctuation-assisted vortex nucleation near the meander's weakest place", while photon counting does not use that path ([Vodolazov et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04689)). A hole wall that serves as both the photon-sensitive edge and the weakest dark site cannot separate them.

**The one positive signal is most likely the ordinary narrow-wire effect, amplified by energy confinement.** Narrow NbN wires saturate earlier. 30 nm wires reach a plateau above a cut-off current I_co ≈ **0.5 I_C** at 1550 nm, while 90 nm wires never reach I_co within the measurable bias range ([Marsili et al. 2011](https://qnn-rle.mit.edu/documents/Marsili-2-2011-84.pdf)). The reciprocal cut-off wavelength scales with wire width in TaN and NbN ([Lusche et al. 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1303.4546)). The zipper is, topologically, three cross-linked 56 nm wires. Its closest experimental relative, the superconducting nanowire avalanche photodetector (SNAP), shows that a hotspot in one branch only diverts current to the others. Detection then needs an avalanche, with I_AV/I_SW of **0.68, 0.78 and 0.84** for 2-, 3- and 4-branch devices ([Marsili et al. 2011, APL](https://qnn-rle.mit.edu/documents/Marsili-1-2011-83.pdf)) and ≈0.56-0.59 in a 2025 series-connected device ([Hong et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02405)). The 12 nm bridges are ~2.4ξ wide, below the ~25 nm (≈4.4ξ) width under which a 2025 study argues vortices cannot form ([Incalza et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18063)). So they act as one-dimensional links that need almost the full cross-section suppressed. That fits their i_det ≈ 0.93 dead-zone behaviour (inference).

**On the dark-count side, narrow channels are expected to be worse, not better.** In 6 nm NbN, the fitted zero-bias vortex barrier scale grows with width: about **1027 K at 53 nm and 1707 K at 171 nm**. The narrow wires also show an extra low-bias dark-count tail below ~0.88 I_c that only vortex-antivortex unbinding explains ([Bartolf et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.1880)). The 20 nm epitaxial NbN wire that saturates well shows "markedly higher DCR and steeper bias dependence" than 30 and 100 nm wires ([Incalza et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18063)). Site counting adds to the penalty. Measured slopes of ln(DCR) = −β(1 − I/I_c), with β = 83-108, mean one decade of dark counts per ~2-3% of I_c ([Vodolazov et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04689)). A hole array that multiplies the number of equally weak sites by 10-1000 would therefore lower the dark-count onset by roughly **2-9% of I_sw** (inference). That is more than the 0.02 gain in i_50.

What is left in the hole family is thin. Semicircular edge notches are as benign as central holes in GL and experiment ([Vodolazov et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.02234)), so they offer no gain. A near-edge hole is the weakest configuration in the dark, and using it as a photon-coupled trigger is speculative. The honest position is that the hole study is a clean negative result. It is worth writing up as one, after the 56 nm control is run, and it does not need more variants.

## Every demonstrated gain protects the weak site instead of adding one

The designs that have widened the window between plateau onset and dark-count onset all share one logic: **strengthen or shield where fluctuations start, and leave the absorber sensitive or make it more so.**

| Design (all *demonstrated*) | Device | Reported gain | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local He-ion irradiation of straights only, bends pristine | 8 nm NbTiN, 250 nm wires | Relative plateau **37% vs 10%** for full irradiation; 94% IDE at 7 mHz DCR (780 nm) ([Strohauer et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11952108)) | No equal-bias DCR comparison of local vs full irradiation |
| Variable thickness: thick bends, thinned straights | NbN, 12 nm thinned to 6 or 4.3 nm | I_sw **+30-40%**; intrinsic DCR **~10× lower** at equal relative bias ([Baghdadi et al. 2021](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2010.11938)) | T_c drop; saturated DE ~10% in one set |
| High-critical-current banks: pristine 1 µm edges, Ar-irradiated centre | 20 µm NbTiN strip | 78% system DE at 1550 nm, ~80 cps DCR; without banks the device switched before saturating ([Yabuno et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06583)) | No plateau width or no-bank DCR numbers given |
| Current-biased Nb rails beside the strip | 3 nm WSi, 5-100 µm | Plateau **+40-44%**; DCR down >9-10 orders (extrapolated); photon count rate changed <1% ([Parzuchowski et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15971)) | Preprint; another paper cites "up to 8 orders" |
| Bend-free single spiral | 5 nm NbN | I_c/I_dep **0.55 vs 0.40** (meander); DCR <10 cps vs ~10³ cps at 0.95 I_c ([Charaev et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.06083)) | Layout change, not a local geometry |
| Optimally rounded bends | ~100 nm NbTiN | Sharp-turn sheet current ~1.7× optimal; DCR cut "by orders of magnitude" at nearly identical photoresponse ([Akhlaghi et al. 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.4290)) | Optimal 180° turns cap fill factor at ~1/3 |

The rails result is the cleanest demonstration that the two thresholds are not fundamentally locked together. Reshaping the edge current cut dark counts by many orders of magnitude while the photon count rate stayed within 1% ([Parzuchowski et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15971)). Field data point the same way. In NbN square spirals, the dark-count minimum (~25 mT) and the 1400 nm photon-count minimum (~17 mT) lie at **opposite field polarities**. The proposed explanation is that dark counts come from vortex *entry* at the inner bend corner, while low-energy photon counts involve vortex *exit* from the hotspot ([Semenov et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.03442)). Theory now gives the floor for this strategy. With edges fully protected, dark counts become limited by bulk vortex-antivortex unbinding, estimated at **I_sw ≈ 0.76 I_dep** for the NIST WSi devices ([Parzuchowski et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.15971)) and **I_c2 ≈ 0.87 I_d/ζ3** in a general model with device-dependent ζ factors ([Gurevich 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02984v1)). Below that floor, no planar geometry helps. Only the material or the photon-to-hotspot conversion can lower the normalised threshold further.

Three warnings apply. First, sensitising a large area can create many new weak sites. In a 2026 NbTiN study that irradiated only the straight segments, the dark-count onset fell from about 10 to 6 µA, and the authors conclude the irradiated segment "dominates the dark-count response" ([Sarkar et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.23277)). Some numbers in that paper look inconsistent (see the last section). Second, edges are the most photon-sensitive sites. Tomography of a 150 nm NbN wire found high local efficiency up to ~40 nm from each edge and a **two-orders-of-magnitude** drop at the centre ([Renema et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.05003)). So strengthening edges can raise I_det for edge absorptions. Third, deliberately asymmetric edges hurt. A field-induced edge imbalance in NbTiN lowered I_det,min but raised I_det,max, eroding the plateau in wider wires ([van der Maas et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10968)).

**The most important caveat concerns transfer to the thesis model.** These gains come mostly from removing a *defect- or crowding-limited* switching current. Measured I_sw/I_dep is **0.43-0.74** in WSi and ~0.69 in NbN, and I_sw is "set by the weakest point along the nanowire" ([Frasca et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.08596)). NbTiN meanders reach only ~60% of a straight wire's I_sw ([Strohauer et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11952108)). The student's ideal strip is already at the depairing limit, and generalised TDGL predicts that crowding penalties are much smaller than London theory suggests, **below 1% at 50% fill factor** for smooth turns ([Jönsson et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05443)). Pearl-length edge crowding is also weak here: w/Λ ≈ 0.03 (inference), and rail-type shaping in strips with w < 0.1Λ needs control currents of **2-10×** the bias current ([Gurevich 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02984v1)). The experimentally proven lever is therefore almost absent from the current model. A credible thesis has to put it back with a realistic defect-limited baseline, and has to measure dark-count suppression with something more sensitive than deterministic I_sw.

## A ranked plan that py-tdgl can run today

py-tdgl already supports what this plan needs. `disorder_epsilon` accepts a callable of position, and time at the `TDGLSolver` level. `terminal_currents` can be a function of time. `seed_solution` restarts from a saved state. Fixed time steps are set with dt_init = dt_max. There is no documented noise or Langevin option ([py-tdgl solver API](https://py-tdgl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/solver.html)). ε(r) = T_c(r)/T − 1 is limited to [−1, 1], vacuum edges and holes use insulating Neumann conditions, and there is no built-in heat equation ([pyTDGL paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.03812)). One practical consequence: ε = 1 is the strongest value allowed, so "strong edges" must be built as **nominal-ε edges around a weakened centre**. The fair reference is then a uniform strip at the weakened value. That is exactly how the bank experiment was made: a pristine rim around an irradiated centre ([Yabuno et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06583)).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | Edge-bank strip: ε = 1 in edge bands b = 10-50 nm (2-10ξ), centre ε_c = 0.5-0.9 | Uniform-ε_c strip and uniform ε = 1 strip, same width | I_sw, I_dep and barrier-based I_dark(R0) of each | Banks, local irradiation, thick bends (table above) | Wins only if I_plateau/I_sw falls versus uniform-ε_c at equal or higher I_sw/I_dep, and the barrier at I_plateau does not fall |

Rank 1 is first because it is cheap, already scripted, and decides the fate of the only positive signal. Rank 2 is the real new geometry. It tests the thesis goal directly and has demonstrated analogues. It also carries a specific risk. In a strip much narrower than Λ the phase gradient is nearly uniform across the width, so a band with lower ε reaches its own depairing condition at a smaller phase gradient than the nominal banks. The weakened centre can then become the new deterministic switching site (inference from GL). This is the TDGL counterpart of both the Sarkar warning and the bulk unbinding floor. If that happens, it is a valid and informative outcome, not a failed run. Map I_det over the full width, because the bank/centre interface can become a new weak line and edge absorptions may lose sensitivity ([Renema et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.05003)). Rank 3 matters because without it rank 2 is being tested in a model where the lever it pulls barely exists. An edge defect acts as a "gate" for vortex entry once the local current near it reaches depairing ([Bezuglyj et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.06467)). Seeding a few of them reproduces the defect-limited regime where the experimental gains appear. Rank 4 is not a geometry, and in TDGL it lowers I_det without touching I_sw by construction. Its value is calibration: it shows how large a purely thermal lever is compared with any geometric one.

**Fair normalisation needs three references, not one.** Report every threshold against (i) the deterministic I_sw of the same geometry, (ii) the depairing current of the narrowest cross-section at its local ε, and (iii) a barrier-defined dark current I_dark(R0), the current at which the whole device reaches a target dark rate R0 such as 1 Hz. A geometry "wins" only if its gain survives all three, and in particular only if I_plateau/I_sw falls at equal I_sw/I_dep. Build the plateau the way published models do. Compute I_det(r) over a full grid of absorption positions, take IDE(I) as the fraction of positions with I_det(r) < I, and define I_plateau = max_r I_det(r) ([Zotova & Vodolazov 2014](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1407.3710)). Then quote the experimental figures of merit: relative plateau width σ_rel = (I_sw − I_onset)/I_sw ([Strohauer et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11952108)) and I_plateau/I_dark(R0). There is room for a small explicit optimisation. No inverse-design or topology-optimisation study of SNSPD electrical geometry for this trade-off was found. TDGL topology optimisation exists only for bulk superconductors ([Deng & Korvink 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.14261v4)). A two-parameter sweep over b and ε_c, with I_dark(R0) − I_plateau as the objective, would therefore be original.

Several ideas should be dropped. More hole patterns are not worth running, for the reasons above. One-sided sawtooth or serrated edges bunch current lines and ease vortex entry ([Cerbu et al. 2013](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/180199)), which lowers the dark onset. No Corbino single-photon detector paper was found, and the notes infer that its inner electrode would become the weak point. SNAP-style parallel channels depend on the external circuit and an avalanche floor that py-tdgl cannot represent. Bilayers are outside a single-layer 2D solver, and the measured WSi bilayer saturated only above I_B/I_SW ≥ 0.9 ([Krapick et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.00004)).

## Three modelling fixes decide whether any conclusion is credible

### Dark counts need barrier heights, not deterministic switching currents

Deterministic I_sw and weak seeds cannot rank geometries by dark counts, because thermally activated switching is far more geometry-sensitive than the instability current. In GL saddle-point calculations at k_BT = 0.1 F0, a straight film switches at ~**0.96 I_c**, while a film with a 2.5ξ-slit bend reaches the same barrier at ~**0.85 I_c** ([Vodolazov 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.0365)). Normalising by deterministic I_sw hides an effect of that size. The fix is to compute ΔF(I) between the uniform state and the saddle at each candidate site: the outer edge, the hole rim, the bank/centre interface. Vodolazov's constrained relaxation does this by pinning a π phase jump at a node to find the vortex saddle, or fixing |ψ| at an edge node to find the vortex-free nucleus. The energy then includes the source work −(ħ/2e)IΔφ ([Vodolazov 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.0365)). A string or nudged-elastic-band method on the discretised GL functional is the alternative. Either requires custom code alongside py-tdgl's finite-volume mesh. The method matters because near I_c the relevant saddle is a vortex-free edge nucleus with ΔF ∝ (1 − I/I_c)^{3/4} ([Skvortsov & Polkin 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18130v1)), and London vortex formulas fail above ~0.6 I_dep ([Vodolazov 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.0365)). A cheap first step uses the student's existing seed bisection. The GL energy cost of the minimal triggering seed is an *upper bound* on ΔF. Variational trial states overshoot the exact barrier by **1.12-3.0×** ([Skvortsov & Polkin 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.18130v1)), so this is good for ranking geometries, not for absolute rates.

The barriers then go into one rate framework for both photons and dark counts: Γ = α·exp(−U/k_BT), detection efficiency SDE = 1 − exp(−∫Γdt), and dark-count rate = Γ with no photon present ([Bauer et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.17177)). Summing over sites captures the site-count penalty of hole arrays. The derived slope can be checked against measured β ≈ 83-108 ([Vodolazov et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.04689)). The absolute scale needs care. By the notes' arithmetic, F0/k_BT at 2 K is ≈2900 with λ = 175 nm but ≈245 with the λ ≈ 600 nm fitted to 9 nm NbTiN hairpins ([Jönsson et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05443)), a factor of ~12. An optional stochastic check is also possible. Add complex Gaussian noise to ψ at a fixed time step with a source-level patch, run a small domain at an elevated effective temperature, and see whether nucleation moves to hole rims. Stochastic 2D TDGL switching statistics are feasible at ~1000 realisations per sweep rate ([Vedin & Lidmar 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19024)). That is useful for checking where switching starts, but too expensive for realistic dark rates.

### The reflecting hotspot needs an energy sink, not a free-space escape

The student should keep reflection but add loss. Published heat and quasiparticle models hold energy in the film at vacuum edges, with zero heat flux at lateral edges ([Zotova & Vodolazov 2012](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1112.3790)) and no quasiparticle loss through side walls ([Engel & Schilling 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1308.5781)). The free-space Gaussian, which lets energy disappear past a wall, has no physical counterpart and underestimates sensitivity near edges and necks. A loss-free reflecting hotspot errs the other way. Once it fills a neck, its energy density decays only through 1D spreading (∝ t^{−1/2} against t^{−1} in 2D), which overweights late triggers in confined geometries (inference). Real films lose energy to phonons and the substrate on ~10-20 ps scales: τ_e-ph = 17 ps in NbN-like modelling ([Zotova & Vodolazov 2012](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1112.3790)), and ~25% of the photon energy goes to the substrate in an ab initio model coupled to pyTDGL ([Simon et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13791)). The timing is close. With D = 0.5 cm²/s, √(Dt) is ≈7 nm at 1 ps and ≈22 nm at 10 ps (inference), so walls 20-30 nm away are reached within 10-20 ps, the same timescale as the loss. A volumetric exp(−t/τ) sink with τ = 5, 10, 20 ps and ∞, applied to both the zipper and the 56 nm wire, settles the confinement question directly. Spot size needs the same care. Near I_c, detection switches on sharply between initial radii of **2.8ξ and 2.9ξ** ([Masaki & Matsueda 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02466v1)). The more rigorous option is to drive ε(t) from a kinetic model, as Simon et al. did with pyTDGL ([Simon et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13791)).

### The detection criterion must tell a passing vortex from a growing domain

Vortices can cross the strip at a current I_vort below the detection current without creating a growing normal domain, and superconductivity then recovers ([Vodolazov 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.06060)). In a GTDGL simulation of a 90° bend, a photon at the inner corner nucleated a vortex at **0.25 j0**, well below the dark critical current of 0.382 j0. The event was slow and weakly dissipative and was judged undetectable ([Berdiyorov et al. 2012](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1206.4298)). A criterion that fires on any voltage pulse therefore inflates the apparent sensitivity of crowded geometries, including hole rims. A "sustained collapse" criterion has the opposite problem. Pure GTDGL lacks the Joule heating that grows the normal domain in coupled models ([Zotova & Vodolazov 2012](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1112.3790)), so its floor is the stability of the GTDGL resistive branch, which depends on γ (default 10) ([pyTDGL paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.03812)). The student should split every threshold into "vortex crossed" and "sustained collapse". They should find the resistive-branch floor by ramping the current down from the resistive state in each geometry, and repeat key thresholds at a second γ.

Near threshold, the response slows down sharply: ~250 t0 (≈10 ps) at a 3.0ξ spot, against under 100 t0 for larger spots ([Masaki & Matsueda 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02466v1)). The student should therefore record the trigger time at every bisection step, flag thresholds that trigger after ~20 ps or near the window end, and rerun with a 2× window. Rims and necks should be meshed at ≤ξ/2, given the 0.25ξ used in careful GL work against the 2ξ maximum edge in pyTDGL SNSPD runs ([Masaki & Matsueda 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.02466v1); [Simon et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.13791)). Convergence should be checked to ~1%. Nucleation must not occur at the ψ = 0 terminals ([py-tdgl solver API](https://py-tdgl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/solver.html)). The plain strip also shows a 1.21× local peak while its mean sits at depairing. That combination is worth a mesh and terminal check, because a true local peak above depairing should not survive.

## What 2D py-tdgl cannot settle, and which numbers to distrust

Some questions are out of reach in this framework. It cannot produce dark-count rates without a source-level noise patch, and even with one, realistic F0/k_BT makes switching a rare event. It assumes uniform thickness, so thick-edge or thick-bend designs can only be approximated through ε(r), which changes condensate density but not sheet inductance. It has no circuit, so SNAP avalanches, latching, readout and rails as separately driven conductors are missing. It is strictly valid near T_c in the dirty limit ([pyTDGL paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2302.03812)), while NbTiN detectors operate far below T_c. Material levers such as the electron-to-phonon heat-capacity ratio, which controls how far I_det can drop below I_dep ([Vodolazov 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.06060)), and thermal boundary conductance enter only through a phenomenological ε(t). Time-dependent applied vector potentials exist but slow the solver, and the maintainer considers screening slow ([py-tdgl issue #31](https://github.com/loganbvh/py-tdgl/issues/31)). That limits dynamic ferromagnet-hybrid tests. Absolute currents are also off scale. The plain strip's ~1.17 mA switching current (inference) is roughly 15× the sheet current of a measured 250 nm × 8 nm NbTiN wire switching near 75-95 µA ([Strohauer et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14965)). That is consistent with λ = 175 nm being too small. Normalised thresholds survive this; absolute currents and absolute barrier rates do not. Finally, real film inhomogeneity is not in the model. The defect-limited baseline is a modelling choice, and the thesis should describe it as one.

## Conclusion

The thesis goal as written cannot be met in the current model, whatever the geometry. If the ideal strip already switches at depairing, "spontaneous switching suppressed over a larger current range" has no deterministic meaning: no geometry can push I_sw higher, and the dark-count differences that matter in experiments sit below I_sw, in the barrier landscape. The goal should be restated as maximising the distance between a barrier-defined dark current I_dark(R0) and the plateau current, measured from a baseline that is defect-limited the way real NbTiN is. Under that restatement the hole result stops being a disappointment and becomes the first half of a coherent story. Adding internal edges moves the weak site but cannot separate it from the sensitive one. Separating them requires protecting the weak site while leaving the absorber sensitive.

The useful lesson for the next step is that the decisive experiment is a comparison of models, not a single geometry. The same edge-bank strip should be run on an ideal and a defect-seeded baseline, with and without hotspot loss, and judged by barrier rather than by I_sw. If it shows a margin only on the defect-seeded baseline, that is still a positive and publishable result. It would be the first TDGL account of why bank and rail devices work, in a niche where the notes found no published hole-array or bank-strip TDGL study at all. If the weakened centre becomes the new switching site even there, the thesis will have reproduced the bulk floor that limits every planar design, and that is equally worth reporting.
